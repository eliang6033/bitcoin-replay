        How the money works

        The gold chart shows Bitcoin’s price, not your bet’s sale price. When Bitcoin rises, Down can become less valuable. A cheap Up bet still pays $0 if Up loses at the finish.

        Market chance comes from the room’s prices. Player counts show how many classmates hold each side. If you hold both, you count once on each side. NPCs trade separately.

        Up wins if Bitcoin ends above its starting price. Down wins if it ends at or below the start. Dollar purchases can include part of a share. Each full winning share pays $10,000 in practice cash, and fractions pay proportionally. A losing share pays $0. You can sell a bet before the finish. Large orders move the room price, so your average price can differ from the displayed chance.

        Choose a percentage, then press Buy Up, Buy Down, Sell Up, or Sell Down. Buy spends that percentage of your available cash. Sell closes that percentage of your holdings on that side. You can hold both sides at the same time.

        Total value includes your available cash and what you would receive by selling all open bets now. Profit or loss is that total minus your starting $100,000. Prices can move before you sell. Buying transfers cash into a bet, so spending $25,000 does not by itself mean a $25,000 loss.

        Buying at 100% spends your full available cash. Selling at 100% closes the entire chosen bet. You can spend only money you have. Combined cash-out accounts for how selling both sides changes the price, so it can differ from adding the separate estimates.

        Dollar purchases spend the displayed amount. If the bet available for that amount drops by more than 3% before execution, you will be asked to try again. Sale estimates can move by up to 3% before execution.

        New rounds have 120 NPCs, each starting with $500,000. Their buys and sales are split into orders of at most $100,000. A $100,000 purchase moves the chance by less than 2 percentage points, before other trades. NPCs can disagree even during a crash. Their trades and market chances are simulated; the Bitcoin price history is real. The leaderboard includes classmates only.

        Class discussion

        Who gets a say when a mine runs overnight?

        In Granbury, Texas, Cheryl Shadden described hearing a Bitcoin mine day and night. In 2024, Earthjustice represented residents seeking a court order to limit the noise and vibration. Their experience raises a question our price chart leaves out.

        At your table

        Imagine a mine offers compensation to run through the night. What condition would you ask for first? Who could enforce it?

        Across the room

        Trade proposals with another table. Which benefits go to miners? Who bears the noise, and what would you need to learn about local electricity and emissions?
